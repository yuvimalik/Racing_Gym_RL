Abstract

The project’s main result is not a single racing champion, but a documented progression from PPO control stabilization to multi-agent safety infrastructure to a world-model diagnosis showing that pose evolution is the next bottleneck.

This report documents a reinforcement-learning project that evolved through a hypothesis-driven experiment loop rather than a single fixed implementation. The project began with PPO control for pixel-based racing and later shifted toward an offline recurrent state-space world model when direct control improvements became bottlenecked by representation quality. Throughout the project, AutoResearch proposed candidate changes, evaluations exposed failure modes, and only runs that survived observation-driven gates were promoted.

As a team, we worked through the project in phases: first stabilizing a single-car PPO baseline, then debugging reward and action-distribution failures, extending the trainer and evaluator to multi-agent racing, building the RSSM world-model branch, and finally consolidating the results into a report, presentation, and executable demo.

The PPO phase established a working visual control baseline after early failures such as steering collapse, off-track reward hacking, and no-progress circling were diagnosed and repaired in the training loop. The strongest early AutoResearch single-car result was `run_008`, which reached mean reward `205.46`, mean progress `0.5551`, and off-track rate `0.036` after `49,152` timesteps. A later recursive control branch promoted `g003_c03`, which combined broader brake exploration with a lower corner target speed and reached mean reward `232.58` with mean progress `0.7376`.

The world-model phase was driven by a different question: whether an RSSM could learn a reusable latent dynamics model of racing rather than merely optimize local control. Fixed-architecture experiments from `E1` through `P5` improved corridor persistence, reward faithfulness, and telemetry prediction, culminating in the stable checkpoint `P5_d4_main_telemetry_a100_bs128_e15`. The decisive improvement sequence inside that ladder was the telemetry pivot `P3 -> P4 -> P5`, which converted the project from generic visual continuation into physics-aware prediction. However, the primary unresolved failure mode remained sharp-turn future dynamics. Later data, objective, and structural ablations were therefore valuable mainly as negative evidence: they demonstrated that curated turn-heavy replay, perceptual or progress-only objective tweaks, a wider stochastic latent, and a lightweight world-motion factorization were insufficient to solve turn evolution.

The central contribution of the repository is thus a transparent benchmark record of hypothesis-driven iteration. The strongest result is not a single headline number but a reproducible chronology: PPO became operational through explicit bug repair and AutoResearch-guided reward tuning, while the world-model branch clarified that future progress most likely requires explicit pose evolution rather than additional fixed-objective training on the existing RSSM family.

1. Hypothesis-Driven Experiment Loop

The project was organized as a repeated Reasoning -> Action -> Observation cycle. In the PPO branch, the cycle was implemented by AutoResearch over candidate training programs and reward-shaping variants. In the world-model branch, the same logic appeared as a sequence of explicit intervention memos, benchmark summaries, and promotion gates. This matters methodologically: the repository is not just a final model archive, but a record of which hypotheses remained credible after simulator evaluation.

Formally, let the research state at iteration k be s(k), the proposed code or configuration change be a(k), and the resulting measured telemetry be o(k). The experiment loop can be summarized as

h(k) = π(s(k)),  a(k) = f(h(k)),  o(k) = Eval(a(k)),  s(k+1) = U(s(k), o(k)).

Here π denotes hypothesis generation, f denotes the concrete intervention, and U denotes promotion, rejection, or reframing after evaluation.

1.1 Scope and Guardrails of AutoResearch

Question Answer in this project
What could it change? Candidate training code, selected reward-shaping terms, PPO hyperparameters, and experiment configs inside the controlled research loop.
What could it not change? The underlying simulator, the requirement to act from image observations, the final human interpretation of results, or the promotion standard for showcase claims.
What were the promotion gates? Runs had to survive evaluation on reward, progress, off-track behavior, steering variance, and later contact/overtake metrics; a high scalar reward alone was not enough.
How were misleading metrics avoided? The evidence base includes reward-hacking cases, visual rollouts, progress deltas, off-track termination, contact metrics, and negative results so a candidate could not be promoted only because it exploited a local reward term.

2. PPO Control Stack

The control branch used PPO on `96 x 96` RGB observations with continuous steering, throttle, and brake actions. The maintained local policy implementation used a tanh-squashed Gaussian actor with corrected log-probabilities, together with separate multilayer perceptrons for the policy and value heads. This separation was not a stylistic preference: it followed directly from an early failure analysis in which a shared head allowed value gradients to corrupt policy features.

PPO optimization followed the standard clipped objective,

LCLIP(θ) = Et[ min(rt(θ) Ât, clip(rt(θ), 1 - ε, 1 + ε) Ât) ],   rt(θ) = exp(log πθ(at | st) - log πθold(at | st)).

Returns were estimated with generalized advantage estimation,

Ât = δt + (γλ) δt+1 + ...,   δt = rt + γ V(st+1) - V(st).

The key project lesson from this stage is that apparently small implementation details such as action squashing, bootstrapping across terminal states, and progress computation determined whether PPO behaved as a driver or as a reward hacker. The strongest control results arose only after those low-level pathologies were resolved.

2.1 PPO Failure Modes: Before, Fix, After

Before Fix After
Steering collapse and zero steering variance after throttle saturation. Per-dimension `log_std` bounds, higher entropy, and separate actor/critic heads to keep value gradients from corrupting policy features. The torch policy retained live steering variance and became stable enough for controlled evaluation.
Off-track reward hacking: high reward while making `0%` real lap progress. Replace velocity-alignment reward with actual tile-progress reward, remove off-track throttle incentives, and terminate off-track episodes. `run_008` reached reward `205.46`, progress `0.5551`, speed `28.32`, and off-track rate `0.036`.
Donut/no-progress behavior survived simple stuck detection. Add no-progress termination and yaw-rate penalties so fast circular motion could not look like successful driving. The later recursive branch `g003_c03` improved corner setup and reached reward `232.58` with progress `0.7376`.

2.2 AutoResearch Pivots

Reasoning Action Observation
`g002_c03` diagnosed persistent `throttle_saturation_no_brake`. Add `reward_shaping.brake_penalty_scale = 0.01`, `apex_decel_reward_scale = 0.2`, and `apex_decel_reward_cap = 2.0`. Progress rose to `0.6662`, but reward stayed negative because the branch remained too dirty.
`g003_c03` reframed the issue as missing brake exploration and late corner setup. Change `ppo.min_log_std` from `-1.0` to `-0.75` and lower `corner_target_speed` from `8.0` to `7.0`. The branch reached reward `232.58` and progress `0.7376`, which became the best documented recursive promotion.

Listing 1. PPO update loop distilled from `train.py` and `autoresearch/train_ppo.py`.

advantages = normalize(advantages)
for _ in range(n_epochs):
    for batch in rollout_buffer:
        values, new_log_probs, entropy = policy.evaluate_actions(
            batch["obs"], batch["actions"]
        )
        ratio = exp(new_log_probs - batch["old_log_probs"])
        unclipped = batch["advantages"] * ratio
        clipped = batch["advantages"] * clamp(
            ratio, 1.0 - clip_range, 1.0 + clip_range
        )
        policy_loss = -min(unclipped, clipped).mean()
        value_loss = mse(values, batch["returns"])
        entropy_loss = -entropy.mean()
        loss = policy_loss + vf_coef * value_loss + ent_coef * entropy_loss
        optimizer.zero_grad()
        loss.backward()
        clip_grad_norm_(policy.parameters(), max_grad_norm)
        optimizer.step()

3. Multi-Agent Bridge

The move from single-car PPO to multi-agent racing was not a simple increase in the number of cars. It required a change in learning topology, data layout, reward semantics, and evaluation. In the maintained torch stack, the relevant paradigm is explicitly `shared_policy_ippo`: one trained network is reused across all cars, each car receives only its own local image observation, and PPO updates are computed from the union of all per-agent trajectories.

That design choice was pragmatic and statistically efficient. The single-agent policy already encoded useful low-level control priors for throttle, steering smoothness, and corner entry. The multi-agent problem therefore did not require a separate controller per vehicle. Instead, it required a way to expose one shared policy to a richer state distribution in which the same local controller sometimes leads, sometimes drafts, sometimes defends position, and sometimes attempts a pass.

Formally, if car i receives observation oi,t and produces action ai,t, then the policy is shared: πθ(ai,t | oi,t) for every agent i. The PPO objective is still the clipped surrogate, but now it is aggregated over all agent streams:

LMARL(θ) = 1 / M Σi=1M Et[LCLIPi,t(θ)],

where M denotes the total number of active agent streams in the rollout buffer. This is precisely how the implementation behaves: with `n_envs` environments and `num_agents` cars per environment, the rollout buffer is allocated over `n_streams = n_envs * num_agents`, and timesteps are counted per stream rather than per environment.

Outcome. The multi-agent branch worked as an infrastructure and evaluation milestone: one shared policy could be trained over batched car-local observations, and the evaluator could distinguish progress, overtakes, rank, contact, hook-contact, and spacing. These results do not yet support a claim of solved competitive racing. The strongest claim is that the project built the machinery needed to study competitive racing safely, while behavior quality remained mixed enough to motivate richer representations and better long-horizon evaluation.

3.1 Architectural Transition: One Car to Many Cars

Single-agent PPO Shared-policy multi-agent PPO Why the change mattered
One observation tensor for one car. Per-step policy batch assembled from all agent-local observations. The network had to process multiple concurrent local racing situations with one parameter set.
One action vector per environment step. One action vector per car, then reassembled into the simulator action layout. The trainer needed explicit flatten/unflatten logic between policy space and environment space.
Reward measures driving competence. Reward must distinguish pace, positioning, safe proximity, and clean overtaking. Naive reuse of the single-agent reward would encourage contact-heavy shortcut behaviors.
Evaluation focuses on progress and staying on track. Evaluation must also measure rank, overtakes, contact, hook events, and front-gap behavior. Multi-agent competence is partly relational; raw reward alone is insufficient.

Listing 2. Shared-policy rollout topology distilled from `train.py`.

n_envs = env.num_envs
n_streams = n_envs * num_agents
buffer = RolloutBuffer(n_steps, n_streams, obs_shape, action_dim)

obs_policy = obs_to_policy_batch(obs)          # one row per car
raw_action, log_prob, value = policy.act(obs_policy)
env_actions = action_batch_to_env(raw_action)  # regroup by environment

buffer.add(
    obs=obs_policy,
    action=raw_action,
    reward=flatten_transition_array(rewards),
    done=flatten_transition_array(dones),
    value=value,
    log_prob=log_prob,
)

At the code level, this transition is visible in three places. First, `TorchPPOTrainer` infers a multi-agent observation/action contract from the environment and validates it before training. Second, `_collect_rollout()` converts the environment state into a batched per-agent policy tensor, queries a single policy, and then maps the resulting per-agent actions back into the simulator layout. Third, reward, `done`, and value tensors are flattened into per-agent streams so that PPO updates treat all cars as training data for one shared policy.

This makes the multi-agent branch a true architectural extension rather than a superficial wrapper. The model is still a single visual actor critic, but the training system around it becomes relational and stream-based.

3.2 What Was Preserved from the Single-Agent Stack

One of the strongest engineering decisions in the repository was to preserve the parts of the single-agent reward that were already doing useful work. The multi-agent config still keeps the control-first prior: strong progress shaping, explicit off-track termination, corner-entry discipline, steering smoothness, and no-progress failure detection. In other words, competition was added on top of competent driving rather than used as a substitute for it.

This design philosophy is explicit in the maintained multi-agent config, which bootstraps from the promoted single-agent control surface `autoresearch_run_008`, preserves `corner_target_speed = 7.0`, keeps steering-smoothness penalties active, and terminates off-track episodes early. From an ML perspective, this is the correct bias: good racing emerges more reliably when control stability is treated as a prerequisite rather than as an emergent side effect of competitive pressure.

The design notes and final implementation converge on the same point: the relevant multi-agent shift is not simply "add an overtake bonus." It is a move from a progress-only reward to a structured competitive objective in which rank incentives are explicitly filtered by spatial safety. In the implementation, overtake reward is tied to pairwise progress-order changes, then softened or suppressed when spacing is poor.

This matters because racing admits pathological equilibria. A naive competitive reward can teach the agent that contact is a shortcut to position change. The repository's later safety rails therefore penalize collisions by severity, escalate prolonged contact into termination, and explicitly track front-gap and side-clearance telemetry. The reward is not merely asking "did the car move forward?" but "did it improve race position in a way that preserves plausible racing behavior?"

3.4 How Overtaking and Safety Were Evaluated

The evaluation protocol had to change as much as the reward. For single-agent PPO, progress, off-track rate, and steering variance were enough to tell whether the policy was useful. For multi-agent racing, those metrics become incomplete because an apparent gain in reward can come from blocking, hooking, or rubbing rather than from an actual clean pass.

The maintained evaluator therefore records competitive and safety metrics directly from per-agent `info` dictionaries. In addition to reward and progress, it tracks `mean_rank`, `mean_overtakes`, `collision_rate`, `contact_rate`, `hook_contact_rate`, `contact_termination_rate`, `mean_max_contact_steps`, `mean_front_gap`, and `min_front_gap`. This is the correct lens for multi-agent RL: we do not only ask whether the cars are faster, but whether they are interacting in a way that deserves to be called racing.

Metric Meaning Why it was necessary
`mean_rank` Average final race position across agents and episodes. Separates mere motion from competitive advancement.
`mean_overtakes` Count of logged overtake events per episode. Measures whether the policy actually changes race order.
`contact_rate` and `collision_rate` Whether the episode contained physical overlap or collision events. Detects the most obvious degeneracy: brute-force contact.
`hook_contact_rate` Frequency of hook-style contact events. Targets a particularly damaging exploit in close racing.
`contact_termination_rate` How often safety rails ended the interaction. Shows whether a “competitive” branch is actually relying on unsafe trajectories.
`mean_max_contact_steps` Average longest sustained contact streak per episode. Distinguishes brief touches from structurally bad leaning or sticking.
`mean_front_gap` and `min_front_gap` Average and minimum longitudinal spacing to the car ahead. Connects overtaking intent to actual available space.

This evaluation design also explains the shift in mentality visible in the project notes: the correct ordering was control and safety first, competition second. Without that ordering, the system can produce superficially aggressive agents that are merely learning to occupy space destructively. With it, multi-agent traversal becomes a genuine extension of the single-agent controller rather than a collapse of it.

For the broader report, the multi-agent branch therefore serves as the bridge between two research questions. It shows how direct PPO had to become relational and safety-aware before dense racing could be studied seriously, and it motivates why a richer world model later became appealing: once interaction structure matters, mere lane-following is no longer a sufficient representation of the task.

Videos 3 and 4 are the key qualitative comparison: they show why better-looking rollouts still need to be judged by whether the imagined camera actually advances through turns.

4. World Modeling Agenda

The world-model branch asked a more structural question than the PPO branch. Instead of learning a direct control policy, the model attempted to compress racing into a latent dynamical system that could reconstruct observations, predict rewards, and carry enough physical structure to support imagination. The implementation centered on an RSSM with an image encoder, a deterministic GRU state, stochastic prior and posterior heads, a decoder, and telemetry predictors for speed, progress delta, steer, corner angle, and off-track probability.

Intuitively, the challenge was not simply to redraw a road. The model had to learn the difference between a stable world and a moving camera. A corridor can remain visually plausible while the imagined ego vehicle fails to progress through a turn in a physically meaningful way. That distinction explains why some later runs looked geometrically tidy but still failed on the curated `sharp_turn` clip.

The sequence model followed the familiar latent-dynamics pattern

Intuitive failure mode. The model can often draw a plausible road corridor, but it does not reliably move the viewpoint through a turn. Sharp-turn pose evolution is therefore the remaining bottleneck, not generic reconstruction quality.
ht = GRU(ht-1, zt-1, at-1),  pφ(zt | ht),  qφ(zt | ht, xt).

Training optimized reconstruction, reward, KL, and auxiliary telemetry losses:

L(φ) = Lrecon + λr Lreward + λKL KL(qφ || pφ) + λtele Ltelemetry + λego Lego.

state = initial_state(batch_size, device)
prev_action = zeros(batch_size, action_dim)
for t in range(time_steps):
    deterministic = sequence_model(
        state.stochastic, prev_action, state.deterministic
    )
    prior = dynamics_model(deterministic)
    posterior = posterior_model(deterministic, encoder(images[:, t]))
    stochastic = sample(posterior)
    reward_t = reward_predictor(deterministic, stochastic)
    telemetry_t = telemetry_predictor(deterministic, stochastic)
    reconstruction_t = decoder(concat(deterministic, stochastic))
    state = RSSMState(deterministic=deterministic, stochastic=stochastic)
    prev_action = actions[:, t]

7. Intuitive Reading of the World-Model Failure Mode

The final world-model story is more subtle than "the model blurs turns." In the strongest checkpoints, the model frequently preserves the road corridor, keeps the ego car visually plausible, and predicts short-horizon reward and telemetry with genuine competence. The failure is narrower: under the `sharp_turn` probe, the model often continues to render a believable scene without advancing the camera through the turn as a driver would actually experience it.

That pattern explains the progression from data interventions to structural interventions. If the decoder were globally poor, more telemetry or more data would have been enough. Instead, the evidence points toward a representational mismatch. The model appears better at describing "what a road looks like next" than "how the agent's viewpoint moves through a turning road." This evidence motivated the later logs to reframe the next experiment around a pose-conditioned branch rather than another broad continuation run.
