Abstract

This project studies how far a reinforcement learning pipeline can be pushed in a visually rich racing simulator, starting from a single-car Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) baseline and then extending toward multi-agent racing and world-model-based control. The first stage established a stable single-car control pipeline using image observations, continuous actions, and a local torch PPO implementation inspired by the clipped-surrogate formulation of PPO [1]. The second stage increased task complexity by scaling to multi-car racing and eventually ten-car shared-policy training, which required changes in environment wrapping, evaluation, anti-contact logic, and experiment management.

The third stage introduced an offline world-model branch based on recurrent state-space models, following the lineage from PlaNet [2] through Dreamer [3], DreamerV2 [4], DreamerV3 [5], and Dreamer 4 [6]. In our codebase, the world-model branch learned coherent short-horizon hallucinations and useful telemetry prediction, but still struggled with hard-turn future dynamics and reliable policy transfer. The strongest outcome is therefore not a single metric, but a coherent research progression: PPO became stable, ten-car scaling exposed infrastructure and interaction challenges, and predictive representation quality emerged as the next major bottleneck.

1. Introduction

The project began with a straightforward but technically demanding question: can an agent learn robust racing behavior directly from raw pixels in a simulator that is visually small but behaviorally unforgiving? Racing is an appealing benchmark because it combines perception, smooth continuous control, anticipation of upcoming turns, recovery after drift, and eventually interaction with other agents. Even the single-car task already contains many of the elements that make visual reinforcement learning brittle in practice: noisy observations, long-horizon credit assignment, and sensitivity to reward shaping and action parameterization.

The work therefore evolved in stages rather than attempting an ambitious end-to-end solution immediately. We first sought a reliable single-car PPO baseline, because without a competent policy it is hard to distinguish optimizer failures from task-definition failures. Once that baseline stabilized, we expanded toward multi-car and ten-car racing, where the problem shifts from pure track-following to contact, ranking, blocking, and overtaking. In parallel, we started a world-model branch to test whether a predictive latent model could capture enough structure about racing to support imagination-based control or, at minimum, provide a more reusable representation than direct policy optimization.

This report is written as a research narrative rather than a changelog. The emphasis is on progression: why PPO remained the practical control baseline, why multi-agent scaling demanded more engineering than expected, and why the world-model branch became attractive despite the practicality of direct PPO. PPO provides the policy-gradient backbone [1]. For latent dynamics, the relevant lineage begins with PlaNet [2], moves through Dreamer-style latent imagination [3], and extends through DreamerV2 and DreamerV3 [4], [5]. For the multi-agent framing, MAPPO-style results demonstrate that PPO-based shared-policy systems can remain surprisingly competitive once implementation details are treated seriously [7].

2. Task and Environment

All phases of the project were built around the multi_car_racing environment, a Gym-style simulator that emits 96x96 RGB observations and expects continuous control actions for steering, throttle, and braking. In the single-car setting, the problem is a visual continuous-control task. In the multi-car setting, the same environment becomes an interaction-heavy racing problem in which ranking, contact persistence, and lane discipline matter as much as raw forward speed.

A useful property of the environment is that it supports consistent evaluation across multiple control paradigms. A direct PPO agent can act online in the simulator, while a world-model pipeline can collect replay from the same substrate and evaluate hallucinations under the same observation semantics. This kept the project grounded on a shared benchmark rather than letting the modeling branches drift apart.

The environment's apparent simplicity is deceptive. The observation is small but visually dense: road boundaries, grass, opponent positions, and ego-car attitude must all be inferred from compressed RGB input. That makes the benchmark useful as a bridge between standard deep RL and more representation-heavy world-model methods. It is complicated enough to motivate latent modeling, but still compact enough for a course-scale project.

3. Single-Car PPO: Establishing a Baseline

The initial phase focused on training a single racing agent from images using PPO [1]. This was the stabilizing step for the entire project, because it answered the most practical early question: can the simulator, reward shaping, and observation pipeline support competent learning at all? If the answer had been no, later work on multi-agent racing or world models would have been premature.

Early versions of the stack followed a standard image-based actor-critic pattern with a convolutional encoder feeding policy and value heads. The project later moved toward a local torch implementation with a tanh-squashed Gaussian policy and corrected log-probabilities under action squashing. That detail mattered: in racing, the agent is sensitive not just to which actions are chosen, but to how the action distribution is represented and optimized under PPO.

This phase also established the importance of instrumentation. The project did not only log reward; it tracked progress, speed, off-track behavior, and evaluation videos. That became essential later, because racing rewards can be deceptive: a policy can exploit local shaping terms without becoming a strong driver. The single-car baseline therefore served not just as an optimizer benchmark, but as the first version of a broader evaluation philosophy.

Methodologically, the single-car phase acted as an ablation filter. Before adding more agents or latent imagination, the project first had to settle practical issues such as action squashing, evaluation cadence, rollout inspection, and run promotion criteria. Many apparent RL improvements disappear once the environment wrapper or evaluation protocol changes; this stage reduced that ambiguity.

4. Scaling from One Car to Multi-Agent and Ten-Car PPO

Once the single-car baseline became usable, the natural next question was whether the same control approach could survive a more complex racing setting. The answer was not simply "increase the number of cars." Multi-agent racing changed the optimization landscape in several ways. Behavior that is acceptable in single-car training becomes pathological once opponents are present: bumping, hooking, lane blocking, and progress-through-contact can all look locally rewarding while producing obviously poor racing.

The codebase therefore moved toward a shared-policy PPO setup closer in spirit to PPO-based multi-agent baselines, informed by the practical lesson from MAPPO-style work that PPO-derived methods often remain stronger than expected in multi-agent settings [7]. Our implementation is not a direct reproduction of MAPPO, because the project remained grounded in a custom simulator and a local torch stack, but the same high-level lesson applied: simple PPO-based systems can stay competitive when the evaluation logic and engineering details are treated seriously.

Relative to the single-car case, the ten-car setting changed both the state distribution and the semantics of success. The policy now had to react not only to track geometry but also to non-stationary visual clutter from opponents, while the training system had to distinguish genuine overtakes from contact-based exploitation. This was the phase where the project most clearly shifted from "train a driver" to "design an experimental system for racing."

The engineering work expanded substantially in this phase. Environment wrapping had to respect per-agent observations and action semantics. Evaluation logic had to report race-specific metrics rather than a single scalar reward. Anti-contact and anti-hooking heuristics became necessary to prevent degenerate competitive behavior. Training infrastructure also became more demanding, which explains the presence of cloud and pod orchestration material in the repository.

5. World Models: From RSSMs to Dreamer-Style Imagination

The world-model branch emerged from a broader scientific motivation. Direct PPO learns a policy, but it does not explicitly learn a reusable predictive representation of the environment. For racing, that limitation matters: the central challenge is not just choosing the next steering command, but understanding track geometry, ego-motion, and future consequences over extended horizons. This makes the world-model literature particularly relevant.

The starting point for our conceptual framing was PlaNet [2], which introduced latent dynamics planning from pixels using a recurrent state-space model with deterministic and stochastic components. Dreamer then shifted from online planning to learning behaviors through latent imagination [3], allowing policy optimization directly inside the learned model. DreamerV2 strengthened this line by using discrete latent representations and showing strong Atari performance inside a world-model framework [4]. DreamerV3 pushed further toward broad-domain robustness, arguing that one world-model RL algorithm with fixed hyperparameters can remain competitive across many task families [5]. Dreamer 4 extends the frontier toward scalable imagination training inside fast and accurate world models, with a more explicit large-scale offline control framing [6].

Our implementation does not attempt to reproduce DreamerV3 or Dreamer 4 directly. Instead, it adopts a narrower course-project path: an offline RSSM-style model trained on replay from the racing simulator, followed by latent actor-critic diagnostics and telemetry-faithfulness evaluation. The repository includes an encoder-decoder backbone, deterministic GRU dynamics, stochastic prior and posterior latent heads, reward prediction, replay collection, hallucination video generation, and a frozen-world-model control wrapper. Over time, the branch also gained telemetry supervision for speed, progress delta, steering, corner angle, and off-track probability.

This pipeline was useful in two distinct ways. First, it produced qualitative artifacts such as side-by-side hallucination videos that made the model's strengths and weaknesses concrete. Second, it supported a more targeted diagnostic lens than direct PPO. Instead of asking only whether the final policy improves, we could ask whether the latent model preserved road geometry, whether reward prediction remained correlated with held out replay, whether off-track classification was faithful, and whether failures were local or structural.

The main technical conclusion of this branch is mixed but informative. Short-horizon straight-line hallucinations became broadly coherent, and telemetry supervision materially improved local faithfulness. However, turn evolution remained the dominant weakness. The project logs repeatedly identify an ego/world inconsistency problem: the model can preserve local appearance and still fail to produce convincing future turning dynamics. This is why the frontier in the repository shifted away from simple loss reweighting and toward more structural interventions around pose evolution and explicit motion conditioning.

6. Results and Lessons Learned

The project produced three main outcomes. First, the single-car PPO branch achieved a stable, executable baseline with meaningful evaluation behavior and enough quality to support demonstration notebooks. Second, the multi-agent branch substantially hardened the training and evaluation infrastructure, even when behavior quality lagged behind the ambition of ten-car racing. Third, the world-model branch produced a functioning offline RSSM pipeline with qualitative and quantitative diagnostics that exposed where model-based control was still failing.

In report form, the strongest result is not a single scalar score. It is the emergence of a coherent experimental arc: from making PPO stable in a visual racing domain, to discovering the engineering demands of multi-agent scaling, to identifying predictive representation quality as the next bottleneck. That arc is what makes the repository suitable for a final-project research narrative.

Several practical lessons followed. Reward and rollout curves alone were not sufficient for racing; visual artifacts, progress metrics, contact metrics, and replay videos all mattered. Scaling the number of agents changed the problem faster than it changed the algorithm: shared-policy PPO remained a reasonable baseline, but evaluation and safety logic became central. World-model progress was also easier to overstate than direct-policy progress; a hallucination video can look compelling while still failing at the future-turn dynamics that actually matter for control.

7. Limitations and Future Work

The single-car branch still reflects the strengths and weaknesses of on-policy visual RL: it can learn strong reactive behavior but does not necessarily yield reusable structure. The multi-agent branch remains difficult to judge without richer competitive metrics and stronger race completion rates. The world-model branch, while technically substantial, has not yet produced the kind of reliable imagined control that would justify replacing direct PPO end to end.

Future work naturally splits in two directions. One direction is pragmatic: continue improving the direct multi-agent PPO branch, especially around contact handling, long-horizon race completion, and evaluation visibility. The other direction is representational: continue the world-model branch with more explicit modeling of viewpoint or pose evolution, improved sharp-turn datasets, and stronger side-by-side diagnostics. These directions are complementary rather than competitive.

8. Reproducibility and Repo Map

The repository already contains the material needed to reproduce the main branches of the project. Training and evaluation entry points live in the root scripts. Colab notebooks provide executable demos. The multi-agent infrastructure is supported by dedicated config files and cloud runbooks. The world-model branch includes progress logs, handoff documents, intervention notes, replay collection utilities, benchmark summaries, and demo generation.

Concretely, the most important entry points are train.py and evaluate.py for PPO, config/multi_car_marl_config.yaml for shared-policy multi-agent training, world_model_train.py and world_model_train_control.py for the world-model branch, and colab_ppo_world_model_demo.ipynb for the executable demonstration notebook.
