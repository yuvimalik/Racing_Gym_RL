Abstract

This project studies how far a reinforcement learning pipeline can be pushed in a visually rich racing simulator, starting from a single-car Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) baseline and then extending toward multi-agent racing and world-model-based control. The first stage established a stable single-car control pipeline using image observations, continuous actions, and a local torch PPO implementation inspired by the clipped-surrogate formulation of PPO [1]. The second stage increased task complexity by scaling to multi-car racing and eventually ten-car shared-policy training, which required changes in environment wrapping, evaluation, anti-contact logic, and experiment management. The third stage introduced an offline world-model branch based on recurrent state-space models (RSSMs), following the lineage from PlaNet [2] through Dreamer [3], DreamerV2 [4], DreamerV3 [5], and Dreamer 4 [6]. In our codebase, the world-model branch learned coherent short-horizon hallucinations and useful telemetry prediction, but still struggled with hard-turn future dynamics and reliable policy transfer. The most important output of the project is therefore not a single metric, but a documented progression of engineering and research choices: what stabilized PPO, what broke when scaling to ten cars, and why predictive representation quality became the central bottleneck.

Project progression at a glance
Phase Question Main outcome
Single-car PPO Can a pixel-based racing policy learn stable lane-following and recovery? Yes; this became the executable control baseline.
Ten-car PPO Can the same pipeline scale to dense interaction and shared-policy racing? Partially; infrastructure matured faster than race quality.
World model Can a learned RSSM capture enough future structure to support imagination-based control? Useful short-horizon prediction, but turn evolution remains the main failure mode.

1. Introduction

The project began with a simple but demanding question: can an agent learn robust racing behavior directly from raw pixels in a simulator that is visually compact but behaviorally unforgiving? Racing is a useful test bed because it combines perception, control smoothness, anticipation of turns, recovery after drift, and eventually interaction with other agents. Even the single-car task already contains many of the difficulties that make visual control brittle in practice: noisy observations, long-horizon credit assignment, and strong sensitivity to reward design and action parameterization.

The work therefore evolved in stages rather than attempting an end-to-end ambitious solution from the start. We first sought a reliable single-car PPO baseline, because without a competent policy it is difficult to distinguish optimizer failures from task-definition failures. Once the baseline became stable, we expanded the problem toward multi-car and ten-car racing, where the challenge shifts from pure lane-following to contact, ranking, blocking, and overtaking. In parallel, we started a world-model branch aimed at a broader question: can a predictive latent model capture enough structure about racing to support imagination-based control or at least provide a more reusable representation than direct policy optimization?

This report is written as a research narrative rather than a changelog. The emphasis is on the progression of ideas: why PPO remained a practical baseline, why scaling to ten cars required more engineering than anticipated, and why the world-model branch became attractive despite the practicality of direct PPO. PPO provides the policy-gradient backbone [1]. For world models, the relevant lineage begins with latent planning from pixels in PlaNet [2], moves through Dreamer-style latent imagination [3], and continues toward broad-domain robust world-model RL in DreamerV2 and DreamerV3 [4], [5]. On the multi-agent side, MAPPO-style results show that PPO-based shared policy training can remain surprisingly competitive in cooperative multi-agent settings [7], which provided a useful conceptual anchor even though our simulator and implementation are custom.

2. Task and Environment

All phases of the project were built around the multi_car_racing environment, a Gym-style simulator that emits 96x96 RGB observations and expects continuous control actions for steering, throttle, and braking. In the single-car setting, the problem is a continuous-control visual RL task. In the multi-car setting, the same environment becomes an interaction-heavy racing problem in which ranking, collisions, contact persistence, and lane discipline matter as much as raw forward speed.

A useful property of this environment is that it supports consistent evaluation across multiple control paradigms. A direct PPO agent can act online in the simulator, while a world-model pipeline can collect replay from the same environment and evaluate hallucinations under the same observation semantics. This kept the project grounded on a common substrate instead of mixing incompatible tasks.

Figure Placeholder A. Environment overview: screenshot grid showing single-car, multi-car, and ten-car layouts.

The environment's apparent simplicity is deceptive. The observation is small but visually dense: road boundaries, grass, opponent positions, and ego-car attitude must all be inferred from compressed RGB input. This makes the benchmark a useful bridge between standard deep RL and representation-heavy world-model methods. It is complicated enough to motivate latent modeling, but still compact enough to iterate on within a course-scale project.

3. Single-Car PPO: Establishing a Baseline

The initial phase focused on training a single racing agent from images using PPO [1]. This was the stabilizing step for the entire project, because it answered the most practical early question: can the simulator, reward shaping, and observation pipeline support competent learning at all? If the answer had been no, later work on multi-agent racing or world models would have been premature.

Early versions of the stack followed a standard image-based actor-critic pattern with a convolutional encoder feeding policy and value heads. The project later moved toward a local torch implementation with a tanh-squashed Gaussian policy and corrected log-probabilities under action squashing. That detail mattered. In practice, a racing agent is sensitive not just to which actions are chosen, but to how the action distribution is represented, clipped, and optimized under PPO.

This phase also established the importance of instrumentation. The project did not only log reward; it tracked progress, speed, off-track behavior, and evaluation videos. That became essential later, because racing rewards can be deceptive: a policy can exploit local shaping terms without becoming a strong driver. The single-car baseline therefore served not just as an optimizer benchmark, but as the first version of a broader evaluation philosophy.

Methodologically, the single-car phase acted as an ablation filter. Before adding more agents or latent imagination, the project first had to settle practical issues such as action squashing, evaluation cadence, rollout inspection, and run promotion criteria. Many apparent RL improvements disappear once the environment wrapper or evaluation protocol changes; this stage reduced that ambiguity.

The outcome was a meaningful baseline policy and a more disciplined training stack. The promoted single-car torch policy became the reference point for later replay collection, policy comparison, and Colab demonstrations. More importantly, it gave the project a working answer to the first-order control problem before the research scope widened.

4. Scaling from One Car to Multi-Agent and Ten-Car PPO

Once the single-car baseline became usable, the natural next question was whether the same control approach could survive a more complex racing setting. The answer was not simply "increase the number of cars." Multi-agent racing changed the optimization landscape in several ways. Behavior that is acceptable in single-car training becomes pathological once opponents are present: bumping, hooking, lane blocking, and progress-through-contact can all look locally rewarding while producing obviously poor racing. Evaluation also becomes harder, because a checkpoint may render successfully while still being strategically useless.

The codebase therefore moved toward a shared-policy PPO setup closer in spirit to PPO-based multi-agent baselines, informed by the literature showing that PPO-derived methods remain stronger than often expected in multi-agent settings [7]. Our implementation is not a direct reproduction of MAPPO, because the project remained grounded in a custom simulator and a local torch stack, but the same lesson applied: simple PPO-based systems can be competitive when implementation and evaluation details are treated seriously.

Relative to the single-car case, the ten-car setting changed both the state distribution and the semantics of success. The policy now had to react not only to track geometry but also to non-stationary visual clutter from opponents, while the training system had to distinguish genuine overtakes from contact-based exploitation. This is the phase where the project most clearly shifted from "train a driver" to "design an experimental system for racing."

The engineering work expanded substantially in this phase. Environment wrapping had to respect per-agent observations and action semantics. Evaluation logic had to report race-specific metrics rather than a single scalar reward. Anti-contact and anti-hooking heuristics became necessary to prevent degenerate competitive behavior. Training infrastructure also became more demanding, which explains the presence of cloud and pod orchestration material in the repository.

The ten-car PPO experiments should therefore be understood as a scale test of the full project stack. They probe whether the repository can support larger populations, whether evaluation artifacts remain usable under many-agent rendering, and whether a shared-policy PPO recipe can learn something better than chaos. Even when the resulting policies are not yet final-showcase quality, this phase materially improved the experimental maturity of the project.

5. World Models: From RSSMs to Dreamer-Style Imagination

The world-model branch emerged from a broader scientific motivation. Direct PPO learns a policy, but it does not explicitly learn a reusable predictive representation of the environment. For racing, that limitation matters: the central challenge is not just choosing the next steering command, but understanding track geometry, ego-motion, and future consequences over extended horizons. This makes the world-model literature particularly relevant.

The starting point for our conceptual framing was PlaNet [2], which introduced latent dynamics planning from pixels using a recurrent state-space model with deterministic and stochastic components. Dreamer then shifted from online planning to learning behaviors through latent imagination [3], allowing policy optimization directly inside the learned model. DreamerV2 strengthened this line by using discrete latent representations and showing strong Atari performance inside a world-model framework [4]. DreamerV3 pushed further toward broad-domain robustness, arguing that one world-model RL algorithm with fixed hyperparameters can remain competitive across many task families [5]. Dreamer 4 extends the frontier toward scalable imagination training inside fast and accurate world models, with an explicit offline-control framing [6].

Our implementation does not attempt to reproduce DreamerV3 or Dreamer 4 directly. Instead, it adopts a narrower but tractable course-project path: an offline RSSM-style model trained on replay from the racing simulator, followed by latent actor-critic diagnostics and telemetry-faithfulness evaluation. Concretely, the repository includes an encoder-decoder backbone, deterministic GRU dynamics, stochastic prior and posterior latent heads, reward prediction, replay collection, hallucination video generation, and a frozen-world-model control wrapper. Over time, the branch also gained telemetry supervision for speed, progress delta, steering, corner angle, and off-track probability.

This pipeline was useful in two distinct ways. First, it produced qualitative artifacts such as side-by-side hallucination videos that made the model's strengths and weaknesses concrete. Second, it supported a more targeted diagnostic lens than direct PPO. Instead of asking only whether the final policy improves, we could ask whether the latent model preserved road geometry, whether reward prediction remained correlated with held out replay, whether off-track classification was faithful, and whether failures were local or structural.

The main technical conclusion of this branch is mixed but informative. Short-horizon straight-line hallucinations became broadly coherent, and telemetry supervision materially improved local faithfulness. However, turn evolution remained the dominant weakness. The project logs repeatedly identify an ego/world inconsistency problem: the model can preserve local appearance and still fail to produce convincing future turning dynamics. This is why the frontier in the repository shifted away from simple loss reweighting and toward more structural interventions around pose evolution and explicit motion conditioning.

DreamerV3 and Dreamer 4 are especially relevant here because they anchor the aspirational direction of the project. DreamerV3 showed that world-model RL can be practical across diverse domains with a single robust recipe [5]. Dreamer 4 raised the bar further by emphasizing scalable, accurate world modeling for imagination training itself [6]. Our project does not claim those capabilities. Instead, it uses the racing simulator as a medium-scale test of the same broader idea: whether predictive latent models can become a stronger substrate for control than direct policy learning alone.

6. Results and Lessons Learned

The project produced three main outcomes. First, the single-car PPO branch achieved a stable, executable baseline with meaningful evaluation behavior and enough quality to support demonstration notebooks. Second, the multi-agent branch substantially hardened the training and evaluation infrastructure, even when behavior quality lagged behind the engineering ambition of ten-car racing. Third, the world-model branch produced a functioning offline RSSM pipeline with qualitative and quantitative diagnostics that exposed where model-based control was still failing.

In report form, the strongest result is not a single scalar score. It is the emergence of a coherent experimental arc: from making PPO stable in a visual racing domain, to discovering the engineering demands of multi-agent scaling, to identifying predictive representation quality as the next bottleneck. That arc is stronger than a disconnected collection of runs, and it is what makes the repository suitable for a final research-style project page.

Several practical lessons followed. Reward and rollout curves alone were not sufficient for racing; visual artifacts, progress metrics, contact metrics, and manually inspected replay videos all mattered. Scaling the number of agents changed the problem faster than it changed the algorithm: shared-policy PPO remained a reasonable baseline, but evaluation and safety logic became central. World-model progress was also easier to overstate than direct-policy progress; a hallucination video can look compelling while still failing at the future-turn dynamics that actually matter for control.

The most important scientific lesson is that the bottleneck shifted over time. Single-car PPO primarily stressed optimization and reward design. Multi-agent PPO stressed scale, safety, and evaluation. The world-model stage stressed whether the model truly understood pose evolution rather than merely reconstructing local appearance. That shift is one of the clearest indicators that the project moved from an implementation exercise into a genuine representation-learning investigation.

7. Limitations and Future Work

The current site draft intentionally omits final embedded media, but the research limitations are more important than the missing assets. The single-car branch still reflects the strengths and weaknesses of on-policy visual RL: it can learn strong reactive behavior but does not necessarily yield reusable structure. The multi-agent branch remains difficult to judge without richer competitive metrics and stronger race completion rates. The world-model branch, while technically substantial, has not yet produced the kind of reliable imagined control that would justify replacing direct PPO end to end.

Future work naturally splits in two directions. One direction is pragmatic: continue improving the direct multi-agent PPO branch, especially around contact handling, long-horizon race completion, and evaluation visibility. The other direction is representational: continue the world-model branch with more explicit modeling of viewpoint or pose evolution, improved sharp-turn datasets, and stronger side-by-side diagnostics. These directions are not mutually exclusive. Strong PPO policies can generate replay for the world-model branch, and world-model diagnostics can in turn clarify which aspects of the racing task direct policies are solving reactively rather than structurally.
